1.1 What Is the Internet? The Internet is a global network of interconnected networks. Devices exchange information through agreed technical rules called protocols. No single computer or company owns the entire Internet; its operation depends on many independently managed networks cooperating.

A home laptop may connect by Wi-Fi to a router, then through an Internet service provider to other networks and finally to a website server. The connection is made of several links rather than one continuous cable between the two devices.

The Internet is infrastructure, not a particular application. Email, messaging, web browsing, streaming, and online gaming all use this infrastructure in different ways.

Organizations use connected systems to coordinate teams, share documents, process orders, and provide customer support. Researchers exchange datasets and publish findings for others to evaluate.

Connectivity also creates responsibilities: users must protect their privacy, verify claims before sharing them, and consider people who lack affordable or reliable access.

1.3 Early Networking and Packet Switching Early computers were often isolated machines. As researchers sought to share expensive computing resources, they investigated ways to connect systems across long distances.

Packet switching divides a message into smaller pieces that can travel across shared communication links. Routers examine addressing information and forward packets toward their destinations. Different packets may encounter different routes or delays.

Packet switching makes efficient use of shared infrastructure, but it does not automatically guarantee delivery. Higher-level protocols can detect missing data and request retransmission when an application requires reliability.

1.4 ARPANET and TCP/IP ARPANET began connecting research institutions in 1969. Its first four sites included UCLA, Stanford Research Institute, UC Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah. It demonstrated practical communication between distant computers.

On October 29, 1969, an attempted remote login from UCLA to SRI initially transmitted only “LO” before a crash. The complete command was sent later. This event is often used to illustrate the experimental beginnings of networked communication.

On January 1, 1983, ARPANET transitioned to TCP/IP. Common protocols allowed distinct networks to interoperate and became a major foundation of the modern Internet.

1.5 Internet and World Wide Web The Internet is the underlying network system. The World Wide Web is a service built on top of it: a collection of linked resources identified by URLs and commonly retrieved with HTTP or HTTPS.

Tim Berners-Lee proposed the Web at CERN in 1989. The combination of web addresses, hypertext links, web servers, and browsers made information easier to discover and navigate.

Email and many games use the Internet without necessarily using webpages. Conversely, opening a webpage requires a network connection to the relevant server unless a copy is stored locally.

1.6 Everyday Internet Applications Common applications include email, search, video calls, cloud storage, online classes, electronic commerce, and entertainment. Each has different performance and security requirements.

For IT students, these differences are useful when designing systems: the same network can support applications with very different expectations about speed, reliability, and privacy.

2.1 Networks, LANs, and WANs A computer network connects devices so they can share information and resources. A local area network (LAN) usually serves a limited area such as a home, classroom, or office.

A wide area network (WAN) connects sites across larger geographical distances. An organization may connect several branch-office LANs through leased links or provider networks.

The Internet connects many networks worldwide. A LAN can operate without Internet access; for example, computers may still share a printer or local file server when the outside connection is unavailable.

2.2 Internet Service Providers An Internet service provider (ISP) supplies connectivity and related services. The ISP connects a customer network to wider networks through its own infrastructure and agreements with other providers.

Residential connections often use a modem or optical network terminal and a router. The access device terminates the provider link, while the router connects the local network to outside destinations.

Service quality depends on the access technology, plan, network congestion, equipment, and distance to remote servers. Advertised speeds are not a guarantee that every website will load at that speed.

2.3 Wired Internet Connections Fiber-optic links carry data using light and can provide high capacity over long distances. Ethernet commonly uses copper cables for reliable local connections between computers, switches, and routers.

Some broadband services use coaxial cable or telephone lines. Their practical speeds depend on the technology deployed and the condition of the local infrastructure.

Wired connections are often preferred for stationary workstations, servers, and equipment that needs stable performance. Proper cabling, connectors, and network configuration are still essential.

2.4 Wireless and Mobile Connections Wi-Fi connects devices to a local network using radio signals. Cellular networks connect mobile devices through an operator’s radio access infrastructure. Satellite systems are useful in some locations where terrestrial service is limited.

Wireless performance changes with signal strength, interference, obstacles, the number of active users, and the capabilities of the device and access point.

Wi-Fi is not itself the Internet: a wireless network may exist even if its router has no working upstream Internet connection.

2.5 Networking Devices A switch connects devices within a local network and forwards Ethernet frames using learned hardware addresses. A router connects networks and forwards IP packets according to routing information.

A wireless access point provides radio connectivity to a wired or routed network. A modem or optical terminal adapts the provider access link to customer equipment.

A firewall applies traffic-control rules. Some consumer devices combine router, switch, access point, and firewall functions in one box, even though these are distinct roles.

2.6 Bandwidth, Throughput, and Latency Bandwidth describes a link’s data-carrying capacity, often expressed in bits per second. Throughput is the rate of useful data actually delivered under real conditions.

Latency is the time taken for data to travel between endpoints. Packet loss occurs when data fails to reach its destination, and jitter describes variation in delay.

A fast connection may still feel sluggish if latency is high or a service is overloaded. Network troubleshooting therefore considers more than a single speed-test number.

3.1 From Requests to Responses A client is a device or program requesting a service. A server is a program or computer that provides a service. One physical computer can run multiple server applications.

When a browser opens a webpage, it identifies the destination, establishes the required connection, sends a request, and processes the server’s response. Additional requests may retrieve images, stylesheets, and scripts.

A modern webpage can contact several servers, including content delivery networks and application APIs. This is why one page load may involve many network exchanges.

3.2 Packets and Encapsulation Data travels through networks in structured units. Application data is wrapped with protocol information so network devices and receiving systems know how to process it.

IP provides addressing and forwarding across networks. Transport protocols such as TCP or UDP provide communication between applications on endpoint devices.

At each link, local framing is used to transmit information over the physical or wireless medium. Network equipment removes or replaces link-layer information as packets move between different links.

3.3 TCP, UDP, and IP Internet Protocol (IP) provides logical addresses and routes packets across interconnected networks. IP does not promise that every packet arrives, arrives once, or arrives in order.

Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) offers an ordered, reliable byte stream by managing acknowledgments, retransmissions, and flow control. User Datagram Protocol (UDP) sends datagrams with less built-in delivery management.

Applications choose transport behavior based on their needs. Some real-time services use UDP, while many web exchanges use TCP or newer transports such as QUIC, which runs over UDP.

3.4 IP Addresses and Local Addressing An IP address identifies a network interface within an IP network. IPv4 addresses are 32 bits, commonly displayed as four decimal numbers. IPv6 addresses are 128 bits and use hexadecimal notation.

Private IPv4 address ranges are commonly used inside home and office networks. Network address translation (NAT) often allows several private devices to share a public IPv4 address.

IP addresses can change. A domain name offers a more memorable way to identify a service, while DNS helps applications find the corresponding network address.

3.5 Domain Name System The Domain Name System (DNS) translates domain names into information such as IP addresses. It is a distributed system, not a single worldwide address book.

When a user enters a website name, the device may check local caches and ask a DNS resolver. The resolver obtains or retrieves the necessary record and returns an answer.

DNS records can include A records for IPv4 addresses, AAAA records for IPv6 addresses, and MX records used for mail routing. DNS lookup and website connection are separate steps.

Deleting a post does not guarantee every copy disappears. Students should consider long-term consequences before publishing information about themselves or others.

7.2 Cloud Computing Models Cloud computing delivers computing resources over networks on demand. Organizations can rent processing, storage, databases, and managed services rather than owning all hardware themselves.

Infrastructure as a Service offers computing infrastructure; Platform as a Service offers managed development environments; Software as a Service provides complete applications to users.

Cloud systems offer flexibility, but users must still manage security, permissions, cost, availability, and backups according to the service model.

7.3 Internet of Things The Internet of Things (IoT) connects physical devices with sensors, software, and communication capabilities. Devices can monitor conditions, exchange data, and sometimes perform actions automatically.

Examples include temperature sensors, smart meters, agricultural monitors, and connected factory equipment. Many systems send data to local gateways or cloud platforms for analysis.

Security is especially important because connected devices may remain in service for years. Updates, unique credentials, and network separation reduce common risks.
